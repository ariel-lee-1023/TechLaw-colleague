# Move 3 — doctrinal-mechanics diagnosis

**Sources**: Dan L. Burk & Mark A. Lemley, *The Patent Crisis and How the Courts Can Solve It* (2009); Mark A. Lemley & Eugene Volokh, “Law, Virtual Reality, and Augmented Reality” (2018)
**Coverage basis**: 20 source chapters/major sections | **Depth**: reference | **Budget**: 5,967 tokens (±10%)
**Load when**: novel facts strain an existing legal category; a technology-specific statute is proposed; a court must choose among analogies or rationales; platform liability, virtual conduct, consent, privacy, or patent tailoring is at issue.

## Mental model: find the joint before designing a new building

Start with the nearest existing doctrine. Break it into elements, rationales, administrability assumptions, and remedial consequences. Then identify the technical fact that changes one of those components. The central question is not whether the technology is unprecedented. It is whether the doctrine's **load-bearing rationale** still supports the same result.

Novel technology usually presents several plausible analogies. Compare what each makes legally relevant, where it fits, where it breaks, and what consequence follows. Prefer an existing **policy lever** when a standard permits fact-sensitive tailoring. New legislation is justified only after showing that the doctrinal toolkit lacks an adequate lever.

## I. General diagnostic method

### 1. Frame at the right level

Avoid “How should the law regulate VR/AI/software?” Identify actor, act, object, injury, jurisdiction, existing claim or defense, and requested remedy. Separate:

- conduct from representation of conduct;
- direct actor from intermediary;
- physical injury from dignitary, informational, economic, and experiential harm;
- factual impossibility from absence of intent;
- legal scope from practical enforceability;
- liability from remedy.

### 2. Decompose the doctrine

For each candidate rule, write:

`elements → underlying rationales → assumptions about the world → institutional competence → remedy`

An element may textually apply while its rationale does not; a rationale may survive while proof or remedy becomes impractical. State which problem exists. “The doctrine does not fit” is too coarse.

### 3. Identify the load-bearing rationale

A rule often rests on several stories. Determine which one actually explains the boundary at issue.

Example: virtual indecent exposure. If the rule principally responds to observers' unwilling exposure and resulting unease, immersive presence can preserve the rationale. If public nudity functions chiefly as a proxy for feared physical attack, a remote avatar weakens it. The same statutory words can produce different results depending on the rationale carrying the weight.

Use legislative text and history, precedent, protected interest, element structure, and remedial design. Do not invent a rationale solely to obtain the desired outcome.

### 4. Build an analogy matrix

For each candidate analogue, compare:

| Dimension | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| actor and control | Who initiated, designed, selected, transmitted, or could stop the event? |
| causal pathway | Does information persuade a person, directly control a device, alter perception, or make physical contact? |
| protected interest | bodily security, autonomy, reputation, property, privacy, speech, competition, or expectation? |
| user choice | informed opt-in, default exposure, ability to filter, ability to withdraw, coercion? |
| scale and persistence | one-to-one, public, replicated, searchable, recorded, or continuous? |
| territorial connection | where are actor, target, server, device, and effect? |
| institutional consequence | what duties, defenses, remedies, and error costs follow from classification? |

The **aeronautical chart versus Mushroom Encyclopedia** contrast illustrates causal pathway. Both are information. A defective chart is integrated into physical navigation and resembles a product “composed in large part of information”; an encyclopedia's erroneous mushroom description communicates ideas that a reader independently acts upon. The analogy turns on functional integration and causation, not the physicality of ink or headset.

### 5. Locate the factual switch

State the smallest fact that changes the result: haptic feedback rather than audiovisual display; default exposure rather than chosen content; platform creation rather than user content; technical indivisibility rather than cheap geofencing; a product containing one patent rather than thousands. A useful analysis tells the reader what additional fact to investigate.

### 6. Separate first exposure from future avoidance

Filtering can prevent repeat harm without erasing an initial intrusion. A user may be able to block an avatar after an encounter, just as a recipient can hang up an indecent call; that does not establish consent to the first event. Analyze defaults, notice, opt-in, speed of self-protection, and whether avoidance imposes the burden on the target.

### 7. Separate doctrinal availability from practical remedy

A claim may exist but be unusable because the offender is anonymous, foreign, judgment-proof, or costly to identify. Lemley and Volokh's **Bangladesh problem** captures cross-border enforcement for low-level virtual harms: jurisdiction and extradition may be theoretically imaginable but practically disproportionate. That gap explains pressure to regulate platforms or build technical self-protection; it does not itself establish intermediary liability.

## II. VR/AR legal map

### 1. Technology changes four legally relevant features

- **Distraction**: AR information competes with physical-world attention and can contribute to collision or injury.
- **Immersion**: VR can produce physiological and emotional effects more like presence than observation, while remaining mediated representation.
- **Image**: avatars separate displayed identity from physical appearance and permit alteration, impersonation, anonymity, and selective perception.
- **Data**: systems must capture motion, gaze, voice, surroundings, interaction, and inferred reaction; the environment is simultaneously an experience and a record controlled by private entities.

Never reason from “virtual” alone. Identify which feature changes the doctrinal analysis.

### 2. Street crimes: harm, self-protection, and sensescape

**Disturbing the peace** raises enforcement and choice questions. Users may technically block disruptive actors, but policing every unwanted stimulus through private filters can burden victims and normalize impunity. The strength of state interest depends on the harm, initial intrusion, available controls, and practical reach over the offender.

**Indecent exposure** sits on the speech/conduct fork. A nude avatar is visual expression like nudity on a screen and also an attempt to simulate bodily presence. Analyze statutory terms, communicative purpose, unwilling audience, immersion, and rationale. Do not let vividness answer the constitutional question.

**Virtual intrusion** challenges property-based categories. Entry into a virtual representation of a place is not automatically physical trespass, yet unauthorized access, circumvention, stalking, privacy invasion, or interference with a system can supply other doctrines. Identify the protected resource and access boundary.

**Strobe-light assault** shows why information/product labels are unstable. Visual output deliberately designed to trigger a seizure has a direct physiological pathway. Intent, knowledge of susceptibility, causation, attempt, and technical control matter more than calling the stimulus “speech.”

**Virtual groping and haptic assault** require separating audiovisual simulation from induced touch. Without haptics there may be no conventional bodily contact, but intentional infliction of emotional distress, harassment, threats, or platform rules may still address harm. With haptics, device-mediated bodily sensation strengthens battery and assault analogies; determine who caused the device to act and what consent covered.

**Extortion and threats** cannot be filtered away when they refer to real-world consequences. Technical avoidance is not a general answer to coercive communication.

**AR startling** creates physical-world risk: an intentionally placed dragon, obstruction, or false direction in a driver's visual field can cause a crash. Analyze intent, foreseeability, product design, user negligence, and causal integration with the physical task.

### 3. Diversity of sensescape

A **sensescape** is the perceptual environment a system presents to a user. Personal filtering can let different users inhabit different versions of a shared space and can support plural norms without one universal prohibition. Its legal promise depends on meaningful choice.

Test:

- Was the relevant environment or stimulus affirmatively selected or merely defaulted?
- Could the user understand the range of expected conduct?
- Could protection be configured before first exposure?
- Does personalization protect the target without falsely representing them to others?
- Are some harms—threats, fraud, real-world coordination, physical injury—unaffected by filtering?
- Does reliance on private controls exclude users who lack skill, information, disability access, or bargaining power?

Sensescape diversity can supplement law; it is not a universal substitute.

### 4. Consent and the magic circle

The **magic circle** describes the bounded play context in which participants accept acts that would be wrongful outside the game. Treat it as evidence of scoped consent, not immunity for anything labeled play.

Consent varies by environment, rules, role, identity, sensation, and time. Entering a combat game may authorize simulated attack under known mechanics; it does not necessarily authorize sexualized contact, undisclosed haptics, doxxing, theft of externally valuable assets, or harm after withdrawal. When play produces real financial, bodily, or reputational consequences, distinguish consent to game mechanics from consent to those consequences.

Impersonation can vitiate or alter consent when identity is material to the interaction. Ask what representation was false, whether the law treats that fact as material, who created it, and whether the participant reasonably relied.

### 5. Direct tort and criminal liability

Candidate causes include assault, battery, intentional infliction of emotional distress, negligence, fraud, defamation, privacy torts, trespass to systems, and specific harassment or cybercrime statutes. Map facts to elements; do not stack labels.

Practical barriers—identity, jurisdiction, proof, damages, and collection—may dominate. Digital records may improve proof while platform control over them creates discovery and privacy problems.

### 6. Operator liability

Pressure to sue identifiable, solvent operators arises when direct actors are unreachable. Keep theories distinct:

- **product/design defect**: the system itself creates unreasonable physical risk, defective warnings, or unsafe defaults;
- **negligent design or undertaking**: operator controls could foreseeably reduce harm, subject to burden and liberty costs;
- **negligent entrustment/supervision**: operator gives access or fails to respond despite relevant knowledge, where doctrine recognizes a duty;
- **vicarious or direct content liability**: depends on operator participation and intermediary-immunity rules;
- **contract**: terms may allocate risk but face assent, unconscionability, public-policy, and necessity limits.

Under the law discussed in the source, **47 U.S.C. § 230** generally limits claims that treat an interactive computer service as publisher or speaker of user-provided information. Do not state current scope without checking current law. Intellectual-property claims have distinct statutory treatment. Ask whether the operator materially created the actionable content, whether the duty is independent of publication, and what relief is sought.

Drafting operators into policing can reduce harm and also expand surveillance, censorship, data retention, and centralized control. Compare technical feasibility, false positives, privacy cost, and less restrictive design.

### 7. Avatars, display, and impersonation

Distinguish private perception from communication to others. A user's private replacement of another person's avatar may resemble private thought or possession. Sharing that representation can trigger defamation, false light, publicity, harassment, or trademark concerns. A user's adoption of another person's identity raises impersonation and source-confusion questions.

For each claim ask: publication to whom, assertion of fact, identifiability, commercial use, consent, parody, confusion, harm, and constitutional protection. The fact that the represented person cannot see the alteration does not mean no legally relevant display occurred.

### 8. Pervasive information capture

Immersive systems can record intimate behavioral and biometric signals: movement, gaze, voice, spatial maps, reactions, associations, and inferred traits. Analyze collection, inference, retention, secondary use, access, security, compelled disclosure, and deletion separately. Terms of use do not exhaust statutory, fiduciary, consumer-protection, employment, or constitutional questions.

### 9. Compulsory VR and privatization

Government-required immersive experiences can be powerful training and also compelled exposure or ideological intervention. Examine purpose, tailoring, bodily and psychological effects, speech and religious objections, alternatives, and evidence.

Most virtual streets will be privately governed. Operators can set speech rules without ordinary First Amendment constraints, subject to specific statutes and possible state constitutional doctrines. Concentration magnifies the consequence of private exclusion. Separate state-action doctrine from the normative question whether dominant environments should bear public-access, competition, due-process, or interoperability duties.

### 10. Order without much law

Private rules, filtering, reputation, technical architecture, and social norms may supply order when public enforcement is costly. Delay can be sensible while technology and norms evolve; premature rules may freeze design. But hands-off governance is itself a distribution of authority to operators and technically sophisticated users. Ask who writes defaults, who lacks exit, which harms remain external, and when experimentation becomes entrenched private government.

## III. Patent-system diagnosis

### 1. The crisis mechanism

Patents address a public-goods problem: invention can be costly to create and cheap to copy. Exclusive rights may encourage investment and disclosure. The same rights can impose search, licensing, litigation, and exclusion costs. The crisis emerges when high volume, uncertain boundaries, uneven examination, expensive litigation, and injunction leverage allow low-quality or obscure rights to tax productive activity.

Key mechanisms:

- **patent flood and overburdened PTO** — limited examination time and incomplete prior art increase error;
- **continuation/do-over dynamics** — applicants can persist and reshape claims while industries evolve;
- **litigation uncertainty** — validity, construction, infringement, damages, and injunction risk are costly to predict;
- **patent holdup** — a right discovered after investment can extract value created by switching costs or product integration rather than by the invention;
- **rational ignorance** — firms in dense patent fields may innovate first because clearance is infeasible, then bargain or litigate later.

Do not conclude that patents are uniformly harmful. Compare benefits and costs within the relevant innovation ecology.

### 2. Diversity of innovation

The unitary statute governs industries that differ along variables directly relevant to patent policy:

- R&D cost and time;
- firm size and financing;
- ease of copying and availability of secrecy, lead time, complementary assets, or regulation as alternative appropriation;
- spillovers and disclosure value;
- discrete versus cumulative innovation;
- number of patents per product;
- product lifecycle relative to patent term and litigation time;
- technical and market risk;
- role of portfolios, cross-licensing, standards, and interoperability.

Build an **industry profile** before selecting doctrine. The relevant category is not merely “software” or “biotech”; identify how these variables operate for the technology and business model at issue.

### 3. Industry-specific patent operation

Patent law is already heterogeneous in practice. Industries differ in propensity to patent, prosecution duration, prior-art sources, claim form, patent-to-product ratio, portfolio strategy, enforcement, and bargaining.

Chemical, pharmaceutical, and biotechnology products may rely on a smaller number of patents tied to expensive development and regulatory processes. Software, electronics, and semiconductors often involve cumulative design, short cycles, and products covered by many overlapping patents. These are tendencies to test, not permanent legal facts.

Intentionality is secondary. Even if courts describe decisions as fact-specific rather than industry-specific, repeated application of standards to recurring industry facts can systematically tailor outcomes. The professional question is whether the tailoring tracks relevant innovation variables and remains transparent and reviewable.

### 4. Competing patent theories as partial industry models

- **classic incentive theory**: exclusion permits inventors to recover investment where copying would otherwise undercut creation.
- **prospect theory**: an early, broad right allows one owner to coordinate development and prevent wasteful rivalry.
- **competitive innovation**: rivalry rather than monopoly drives firms to innovate; strong rights can suppress that pressure.
- **cumulative innovation**: later advances build on earlier ones, requiring a division of value and access between pioneers and improvers.
- **anticommons**: fragmented rights over complementary inputs create transaction and holdout barriers.
- **patent thickets**: overlapping rights around products create clearance, bargaining, and litigation costs.

The theories conflict when treated as universal accounts. Treat each as a model whose factual predicates fit some industries better than others. Ask whether invention is discrete or cumulative, centralized or distributed, capital-intensive or rapid and incremental, and whether coordination problems arise from too little control or too many rights.

## IV. Courts, Congress, and policy levers

### 1. Why not specialty statutes by default

Industry-specific legislation appears direct but invites boundary disputes, lobbying, rent-seeking, ossification, and **balkanization** of a nominally general system. Technological categories change faster than statutes and firms can characterize inventions strategically to enter favorable regimes. Broad legislative reform can also stall because industries need opposing prescriptions.

This does not mean courts are always superior. Congress should set distributional choices, create new institutions, or alter clear statutory commands. The narrower claim is that flexible standards often already authorize courts to respond to technology-specific facts without separate codes.

### 2. Rules, standards, and institutional competence

Standards permit contextual adjustment but can reduce notice and consistency. Courts see concrete disputes and can update incrementally; they also depend on party records, face technical complexity, and may generate path dependence. Use a lever only when its text and rationale support the adjustment. Explain the relevant fact so tailoring does not become unreviewable intuition.

### 3. Existing acquisition and validity levers

- **PHOSITA / level of ordinary skill in the art**: calibrates what knowledge and capability doctrine attributes to the field. It affects obviousness, enablement, and interpretation. Define education, experience, tools, collaboration, and pace; do not use “skilled artisan” as a conclusion.
- **nonobviousness**: tests whether the claimed advance exceeds what the field would have produced from prior art. Account for field predictability, motivations to combine, simultaneous invention, and secondary considerations without mechanically rewarding hindsight.
- **enablement**: calibrates how much teaching the patent must provide across claim scope. Unpredictable arts may require more examples and narrower claims; predictable arts may require less, but functional breadth can still outrun disclosure.
- **written description**: asks whether the application shows possession of the claimed invention, constraining later claims that capture developments not actually disclosed.
- **experimental use and statutory bars**: definitions can distinguish development practices and timing; avoid punishing necessary experimentation.
- **presumption of validity**: evidentiary weight can respond to whether the PTO considered the most relevant prior art, if doctrine permits.

### 4. Scope levers

- **claim construction and abstract ideas**: prevent claims from capturing a principle at a level untethered to a disclosed implementation, while avoiding categorical hostility to a field.
- **reasonable interchangeability and doctrine of equivalents**: calibrate whether substitutes count as equivalents in light of predictability and what a skilled person would understand.
- **element-by-element analysis**: prevents overall similarity from erasing claim limits.
- **pioneering patents**: scope may reflect the nature of the contribution, but “pioneer” requires evidence and cannot excuse disclosure limits.
- **reverse doctrine of equivalents**: conceptually limits literal coverage when an accused technology operates on a substantially different principle; its rarity makes it a cautious lever, not a routine escape.
- **patent misuse**: can police attempts to extend leverage beyond the patent's legitimate scope, subject to current statutory and doctrinal limits.

### 5. Remedy levers

- **injunction**: test irreparable harm, adequacy of damages, equities, and public interest; avoid allowing a small component patent to hold up an entire complex product merely because exclusion is the formal right.
- **reasonable royalty**: isolate the patented contribution from value created by standardization, switching costs, complementary features, and litigation leverage. Choose an appropriate royalty base and bargaining frame.
- **property versus liability rule**: exclusion and payment-only remedies create different bargaining positions. Select through governing doctrine, not a general preference for ownership.

Remedy tailoring can address holdup without denying validity. Keep the liability finding, scope determination, and remedy analysis distinct.

### 6. Biotechnology and IT profiles

**Biotechnology** often combines expensive and uncertain development, longer timelines, regulatory cost, sequence-based disclosure questions, and relatively discrete therapeutic products. Courts have used enablement and written description stringently, sometimes producing narrow protection. Ask whether that tailoring supplies adequate incentives or fragments foundational research.

**Software and IT** often feature rapid cumulative innovation, distributed prior art, functional claiming, short product cycles, many patents per product, portfolios, interoperability, and holdup risk. Levers around obviousness, disclosure, abstractness, scope, injunctions, and royalties can reduce mismatch. Do not assume all software is cheap, incremental, or non-disclosed; profile the actual sector.

Semiconductors add capital intensity, dense portfolios, cross-licensing, standards, and reverse-engineering practices. A shared “IT” label can obscure meaningful differences.

## V. Decision rules

- When a novel artifact carries information into physical action, compare its causal integration with a chart and with an encyclopedia.
- When immersive conduct is vivid, identify whether the claimed harm is bodily, experiential, dignitary, informational, economic, or reputational before classifying it.
- When filtering is offered as the answer, test default settings, first exposure, informed choice, unequal burden, and harms filtering cannot prevent.
- When game participation is called consent, define the magic circle's scope, rules, identity conditions, exit, and real-world spillovers.
- When a direct wrongdoer is hard to reach, do not leap to platform liability; map duty, control, knowledge, immunity, privacy, speech, and design alternatives.
- When digital identity is altered, separate private perception, publication, impersonation, commercial use, and source confusion.
- When a patent dispute is framed as industry-specific, replace the label with an innovation profile.
- When patent theories conflict, test their factual predicates rather than selecting one as universally correct.
- When a general standard contains a policy lever, tailor transparently through the relevant fact and limiting principle before seeking a specialty statute.
- When holdup drives concern, keep validity, scope, injunction, royalty base, and apportionment analytically separate.
- When current law matters, verify statutes and precedent; these sources supply analytical structure, not a 2026 authority update.
