# Move 2 — efficiency refusal and human expertise

**Source**: Frank Pasquale, *New Laws of Robotics: Defending Human Expertise in the Age of AI* (2020)
**Coverage basis**: 8 source chapters | **Depth**: reference | **Budget**: 4,611 tokens (±10%)
**Load when**: automation is defended as cheaper, scalable, objective, personalized, or inevitable; a system substitutes for professional judgment; an artificial agent imitates a person; automated ranking judges people; or competitive pressure is offered as a reason to deploy.

## Mental model: govern the makers, not an imagined machine will

The “new laws” address the people and institutions that design, own, buy, and deploy robotics and AI. They are broad principles for sociotechnical choices, not executable rules for autonomous machines. The governing contrast is not human versus technology. It is technology organized to **complement** human capability versus technology organized to **replace**, counterfeit, regiment, or evade responsibility.

Efficiency is therefore an intermediate fact, never the conclusion. Ask what is being optimized, who selected the metric, whose work or welfare disappears from it, who captures the gain, and who bears error and transition costs. A choice remains political when a machine can technically perform it.

## I. Four new laws of robotics

### 1. Robotic systems and AI should complement professionals, not replace them

Automate tasks that increase workers' competence, productivity, safety, discretion, or capacity to serve others. Do not infer that because a task can be modeled, the professional role should be liquidated. A profession integrates formal knowledge with situated observation, responsibility, trust, explanation, and judgment about ends. Those elements often determine what the task is.

Use **task decomposition**:

1. Identify the claimed automatable task narrowly.
2. Identify upstream work that makes the data meaningful and downstream work that handles ambiguity, exceptions, explanation, and care.
3. Ask whether automation transfers knowledge to the professional or transfers authority away from them.
4. Test deskilling and the **pipeline problem**: if entry-level tasks disappear, how will future experts acquire experience and improve the system?
5. Measure effects on job quality, bargaining power, service quality, and access—not only labor hours.

Complementarity is not satisfied merely by leaving a person “in the loop.” A human who rubber-stamps an opaque recommendation, bears liability without power, or intervenes only after irreversible harm is a responsibility sink, not a professional complement.

### 2. Robotic systems and AI should not counterfeit humanity

Distinguish **humanizing technology** from **counterfeiting human characteristics**. Polite, legible interfaces can aid use. Deliberately causing a person to believe a machine possesses human identity, attention, care, distress, loyalty, or independent judgment exploits social expectations it cannot reciprocate.

Counterfeiting is especially serious where vulnerability and trust are constitutive: therapy, elder care, teaching, medicine, intimate companionship, public authority, and political persuasion. Evaluate:

- whether identity is disclosed at the moment it matters;
- whether voice, hesitation, affect, or embodiment is designed to induce mistaken human attribution;
- whether the system claims empathy or concern while optimizing another party's commercial objective;
- whether users can understand whose interests the agent serves;
- whether simulation crowds out accessible human care or advice.

Passing a behavioral imitation test does not settle whether substitution is desirable. The ethical question includes the relationship, provenance, purpose, and asymmetry behind the performance.

### 3. Robotic systems and AI should not intensify zero-sum arms races

Do not treat “others will deploy it” as self-justifying. In military, policing, surveillance, ranking, advertising, finance, and workplace monitoring, one actor's automation can pressure every rival to automate, even when all would prefer a constrained equilibrium. Speed can shorten deliberation, multiply error, and make escalation harder to reverse.

Test for an arms race:

- Does non-adoption create a strategic disadvantage independent of social value?
- Does automation increase tempo or scale beyond meaningful human review?
- Do benefits accrue privately while systemic risks are externalized?
- Can verification, procurement standards, interoperability rules, professional norms, treaties, moratoria, or liability support cooperation?
- Would making deployment costlier improve collective security or merely shift advantage to less accountable actors?

The answer need not be prohibition. The rule requires designing institutions that prevent competitive pressure from becoming destiny.

### 4. Robotic systems and AI must always indicate the identity of their creators, controllers, and owners

Attribution is more than a label on a website. Responsibility should trace through the system's lifecycle to actors able to prevent, monitor, explain, insure, compensate, recall, or stop it. Machine learning, adaptation, and chains of generated code do not dissolve ownership or control.

Create an **accountability map**:

| Role | Question |
| --- | --- |
| creator/model developer | Who selected architecture, training objectives, data, and foreseeable limits? |
| deployer | Who chose the use, population, threshold, safeguards, and integration into practice? |
| controller/operator | Who can monitor, override, suspend, update, or withdraw the system? |
| owner/beneficiary | Who captures revenue, savings, strategic advantage, or data? |
| professional/institution | Who owes duties to the affected person independent of the vendor contract? |

Contractual allocation does not settle public responsibility. Reject four recurring “horsemen” of irresponsibility: the maker calls the tool a neutral blank slate; the deployer blames the model; the professional claims compelled reliance; and every actor points to unpredictable autonomy. Assign duties to the actor best positioned to manage each risk while preserving joint responsibility where choices compound.

## II. Professionalism and the two crises of expertise

Expertise is not possession of facts alone. It operates where science and technology meet law, democratic contestation, and local knowledge. Professional judgment translates general knowledge into particular circumstances, recognizes what is missing, and remains answerable for a decision.

The first crisis is technocratic overreach: experts can become insulated, self-serving, unequal, or blind to affected communities. The second is indiscriminate anti-expert reaction: distrust of institutions becomes a warrant to replace accountable professions with proprietary systems that are even less contestable. The response is **democratized and accountable expertise**, not expert abolition.

Evaluate a professional system by whether it:

- incorporates affected communities without pretending every technical question is a referendum;
- exposes conflicts, error rates, and reasons to peer and public challenge;
- preserves discretion for justified exceptions while monitoring unjustified variation;
- distributes high-quality service rather than reserving human expertise for elites and automated substitutes for everyone else;
- develops institutional memory and future practitioners.

Variation is neither automatically wisdom nor automatically bias. Extreme divergence can reveal poor practice; standardized metrics can also erase relevant difference. Compare reasons, not uniformity alone.

## III. Domain modules

### 1. Healing humans

AI's core competence in medicine is often narrow pattern recognition that can help avoid common errors. Treat it as one component of a **learning health-care system**, not an autonomous diagnostician detached from clinicians, patients, and institutions.

Audit:

- data representation across populations, institutions, equipment, and disease prevalence;
- calibration and failure outside the training environment;
- whether clinicians can contest and improve recommendations;
- whether alerts support attention or produce fatigue and automation bias;
- whether personalization expands care or fragments responsibility;
- the therapy app's business model, confidentiality, evidence base, escalation path, and true client.

“Better on average” does not mean appropriate for every patient. Unequal data can reproduce disparities, and a low-cost automated service can become a lower tier offered to people denied access to professionals. In care work, presence, touch, conversation, and noticing changes are productive activities even when throughput metrics classify them as delay.

### 2. Beyond machine learners: education

Education has plural purposes: knowledge, capability, civic formation, social development, judgment, and the experience of learning with others. A simple neoliberal vision reduces it to measurable human-capital outputs and treats teaching as content delivery.

Ask of educational technology:

- Who is the teaching robot working for: student, teacher, school, parent, vendor, or future employer?
- Does personalization expand a teacher's ability to respond, or isolate students in behaviorist feedback loops?
- Does continuous attention monitoring improve learning or normalize surveillance and obedience?
- Who may inspect, correct, transfer, and delete the learner model?
- Does the system create time for teachers to teach, or convert them into proctors for a vendor's metrics?

Prefer robotic helpers that remove unsafe or mechanical burdens, make disability accommodations, support practice, and return interpretable information to educators. Resist the **new behaviorism** in which every action becomes a measurable stimulus-response opportunity and education's unquantified ends disappear.

### 3. The alien intelligence of automated media

Automated media systems are not neutral mirrors of audience preference. Ranking, recommendation, advertising, and moderation create an attention environment optimized for platform objectives. Scale and opacity let owners disclaim responsibility for outcomes their architecture predictably amplifies.

The diagnosis has three parts:

1. **Absent ownership responsibility** — firms characterize harmful content as users' speech while controlling amplification, monetization, targeting, and design.
2. **Wrecking ball of online attention** — optimization for engagement can reward outrage, manipulation, and compulsive use.
3. **Algorithmic-governance limit** — legitimate public-sphere judgments require contestable standards, institutions, and professional responsibility; no ranking metric can settle inclusion and justice by itself.

Restore responsibility through transparency about objectives and sponsorship, auditable processes, meaningful appeals, limits on behavioral targeting, and professional editorial commitments. The goal is not nostalgia for infallible gatekeepers, but accountable mediation.

### 4. Machines judging humans

Scores promise consistency, speed, and inclusion but can convert partial proxies into total judgments. **Misrecognition as disrespect** occurs when a system assigns an identity, risk, merit, or intention that the person cannot meaningfully contest. The injury is not exhausted by statistical error; it includes being governed through an alien and reductive representation.

For credit, employment, insurance, education, welfare, policing, or sentencing, ask:

- What is the target construct, and is the observable proxy valid?
- Which historical conditions produced the training label?
- Does expanded inclusion expose vulnerable people to predatory pricing or surveillance?
- Can the subject know the material reasons, correct data, challenge the model, and obtain human reconsideration?
- Does scoring induce people to internalize obedience to opaque criteria?
- Can institutions be scored from below by workers, customers, and affected communities?

Turn the tables on judgmental AI: evaluate the evaluator's power, objectives, error distribution, and accountability. “Objective” should mean a claim open to validation, not an aura conferred by computation.

### 5. Autonomous forces

Automation can make force easier to initiate, faster to escalate, and harder to attribute. The pro-automation case—precision, force protection, consistent compliance—must be weighed against identification error, context loss, proliferation, cyber vulnerability, lowered political cost of violence, and arms-race dynamics.

Preserve meaningful human control where legal and moral judgment cannot be reduced to target recognition. Responsibility must include commanders, procuring institutions, designers, operators, and political leaders. The **logic of cooperation** asks how reciprocal restraint can be stabilized despite mistrust; barriers to bans are reasons to design verification and narrower agreements, not reasons to abandon restraint.

### 6. Political economy of automation

Firm-level incentives favor labor substitution because wages appear as costs. At the social level, income from work sustains demand and gives people standing, security, and participation. “What consumers spend, workers earn” captures the macro connection that a balance sheet omits.

Policy must move from job training alone to **innovation governance**: shape which technologies receive finance, tax preference, procurement, infrastructure, and legal permission. Apply **subsidiarity** by locating decisions at the most local level capable of handling them while supporting public capacity above it.

Do not assume universal basic income answers the loss of work, services, bargaining power, and social infrastructure. A **job guarantee** is a distinct proposal to finance needed work, especially care, education, maintenance, and ecological service. The point is not that one instrument always wins; it is that cash compensation cannot by itself govern the direction of innovation.

Challenge the **cost disease** frame when rising expenditure in labor-intensive services reflects higher wages, better access, or more care rather than waste. Purposeful inefficiency—the caregiver who stays, listens, and notices—is sometimes the service's substance. Public finance is constrained by real resources, institutional capacity, inflation, and distribution, not a household-budget metaphor alone.

### 7. Computational power and human wisdom

Automation ideology is cultural as well as economic. Narratives of interchangeability and machine superiority can make replacement appear natural before institutions choose it. Embodied human minds bring vulnerability, context, ambiguity, and social history that abstract information processors do not reproduce simply by generating persuasive outputs.

Authenticity in art, care, and judgment concerns more than the artifact's surface. It includes a creator's situation, struggle, intention, relationships, and the audience's participation in a social practice. This does not deny machine-assisted creativity; it rejects the inference that behavioral similarity erases provenance and power.

Choose **more AI** versus **better AI** deliberately. Better AI supports maintenance, accountable institutions, shared capability, and human purposes. More AI merely increases scale, prediction, and substitution.

## IV. The benefit of cost

Cost is not automatically waste. Payment for a service is also income, capability, and social investment. Time spent explaining, deliberating, checking, teaching, or caring may be the mechanism that produces trust and quality. The relevant distinction is between avoidable burden and constitutive human effort.

Use a cost ledger:

| Claimed saving | Ask what may be lost | Ask who receives the gain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fewer labor hours | experience, attention, future expertise, resilience | owners, purchasers, consumers, or public budgets |
| faster decision | deliberation, explanation, exception handling, appeal | high-volume deployer |
| standardized service | local knowledge, justified discretion, relational care | vendor and central manager |
| automated access | privacy, quality, escalation, equal access to humans | platform and data broker |
| predictive accuracy | autonomy, contestability, non-measured values | institution exercising judgment |

Concede genuine savings explicitly. Then decide whether the saved resource was waste, a cross-subsidy, a safeguard, or the point of the practice.

## V. Operational review protocol

1. **Name the deployment choice**: who proposes what system, for which task, population, institution, and decision?
2. **Decompose the work**: separate automatable components from judgment, care, explanation, exception handling, and learning.
3. **Identify the optimization target**: metric, objective, sponsor, excluded values, and distribution of errors.
4. **Classify the mode**: complement, replace, counterfeit, race-intensify, or responsibility-obscure. More than one may apply.
5. **Map responsibility**: creators, deployers, controllers, owners, professionals, beneficiaries, and affected people.
6. **Examine power**: authority, information, bargaining position, surveillance, deskilling, and exit options before and after deployment.
7. **Test contestability**: notice, intelligible reasons, correction, appeal, human reconsideration, audit, and capacity to halt.
8. **Run the cost ledger**: admit benefits, then locate displaced costs and lost capabilities.
9. **Design complementarity**: specify which decisions remain human, what information the system returns, and how professionals can challenge and improve it.
10. **Check the long term**: expertise pipeline, institutional resilience, market concentration, arms-race pressure, and alternative public investment.

## VI. Decision rules

- When efficiency is the argument, ask “efficient for whom, by which metric, over what horizon, and with which costs omitted?”
- When replacement is proposed, decompose tasks and preserve professional authority over ends, exceptions, explanation, and learning.
- When a human remains in the loop, verify time, information, competence, power to override, and protection from retaliation.
- When a system imitates a person, require timely identity, sponsor, purpose, and limits; vulnerability raises the disclosure burden.
- When competitive necessity is invoked, test for an arms race and search for cooperative restraints.
- When autonomy is used to deny liability, trace creator, deployer, controller, and owner; unpredictability reallocates duties but does not erase them.
- When performance is “better on average,” examine distribution, context shift, error remedy, and unequal access to humans.
- When standardization reduces variation, distinguish correction of arbitrary practice from destruction of justified discretion.
- When automation saves labor in care or education, test whether the removed time was waste or the service itself.
- When deciding between more and better AI, prefer accountable augmentation, maintenance, and shared capability over scale for its own sake.
