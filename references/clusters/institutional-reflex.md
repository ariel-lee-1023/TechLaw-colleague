# Move 1 — comparative-institutional diagnosis

**Sources**: Anu Bradford, *The Brussels Effect* (2020) and *Digital Empires* (2023)
**Coverage basis**: 18 source chapters | **Depth**: reference | **Budget**: 5,778 tokens (±10%)
**Load when**: the question asks why a jurisdiction regulates technology as it does; whether a rule will travel; how governments, firms, and rival states divide power; or what a regulatory design reveals before it is evaluated.

## Mental model: regulation is both rule and projection of power

Begin with the political order that produced the rule. A technology regulation is not merely a response to a technical risk. It reflects a jurisdiction's view of markets, state authority, rights, expertise, and legitimate coercion. Its reach outside the jurisdiction is then a second question: which institutional and market mechanisms can turn a domestic preference into a foreign firm's practice or another state's law?

Keep three levels separate:

1. **Domestic model** — which actor is presumptively trusted to order the digital economy, and which value receives priority?
2. **Rivalry** — how do governments contest one another horizontally while each fights vertically with technology companies?
3. **Expansion** — through what carrier does a domestic model reach foreign societies: firms, infrastructure, or regulation?

Do not infer virtue from reach. Effectiveness is an empirical conclusion; legitimacy and welfare are separate judgments. A mechanism can protect rights, impose costs, override foreign choices, and strengthen incumbents at the same time.

## I. The three regulatory models

The models are ideal types, not claims that every rule in a jurisdiction is uniform. Use them to identify the default allocation of authority and the characteristic justification for intervention or restraint.

### 1. American market-driven regulatory model

- **Core allocation**: private firms and competitive markets receive the initial freedom to shape the digital environment. Government intervention bears the burden of justification.
- **Intellectual roots**: techno-libertarianism, distrust of government, confidence in permissionless innovation, and the idea that code and market choice can solve problems more flexibly than public rules.
- **Rights emphasis**: free speech is often treated as the preeminent digital right; privacy, equality, and collective interests receive less categorical protection when they conflict with speech or innovation.
- **Legal embedding**: limited intermediary liability, industry self-regulation, antitrust focused historically on prices and consumer welfare, and tolerance of data-driven business models enabled firms to accumulate private power.
- **Productive strength**: capital, talent, research institutions, deep markets, and large technology companies can generate rapid innovation and global adoption.
- **Internal contradiction**: a model justified as dispersing state power can concentrate quasi-governmental power in platforms that govern speech, commerce, labor, identity, and access without ordinary public-law accountability.
- **Current stress**: surveillance capitalism, market concentration, disinformation, and platforms' influence over democracy have weakened the model's normative appeal. A change in rhetoric does not itself establish a change in legislation, enforcement, or judicial doctrine.

### 2. Chinese state-driven regulatory model

- **Core allocation**: the state remains supreme over firms, networks, and users. Markets and technological development are instruments of national development and regime stability, not autonomous sources of authority.
- **Dual objective**: technological self-sufficiency and economic growth operate alongside censorship, surveillance, propaganda, and social control. Innovation and authoritarian control can coexist; do not assume political freedom is an empirical prerequisite for every kind of technological advance.
- **Digital protectionism**: restrictions on foreign firms and information flows can shelter domestic champions while ensuring that the digital sphere remains governable by the state.
- **Party-state supremacy**: apparent tolerance of private technological power is conditional. Crackdowns framed around security or “common prosperity” demonstrate that companies do not outrank the governing order.
- **External carrier**: the **Digital Silk Road** exports equipment, standards, finance, training, and integrated systems. Infrastructure supplies a durable channel of influence because later technical choices, maintenance, data practices, and governance capacities depend on the installed stack.
- **Normative cost**: the model can supply infrastructure and development that recipient states want, yet enable surveillance and censorship, strengthen authoritarian incumbents, and create dependency.

### 3. European rights-driven regulatory model

- **Core allocation**: digital markets are legitimate only within public rules that protect individual and collective rights. Regulation is a constitutive feature of the market, not an exceptional correction after failure.
- **Rights portfolio**: privacy, data protection, dignity, equality, consumer protection, fair competition, worker protection, media pluralism, and democratic integrity are balanced rather than subordinated categorically to speech or growth.
- **Institutional method**: binding rules, specialized administration, judicial supervision, and market-wide harmonization. The EU treats digital transformation as something to anchor in law and democratic institutions.
- **Integration function**: regulation builds the internal market by replacing divergent national standards with common ones. High-regulation member states often support **upward harmonization** so their firms are not disadvantaged by lower standards elsewhere.
- **Distributional ambition**: digital regulation can move power away from dominant platforms toward users, workers, smaller firms, and public institutions.
- **Internal weaknesses**: compliance cost may burden smaller firms; fragmented capital and product markets, risk aversion, limited scale-up finance, and uneven enforcement can inhibit European firms independently of regulation. Do not attribute every innovation gap to regulatory stringency.
- **Vertical-battle problem**: sophisticated law without enforcement capacity can leave platform power intact. A rights-driven rule on paper is not a rights-driven outcome.

### 4. Model selection and hybridization

No state applies one model without qualification. The United States uses export controls and industrial subsidies; China uses competitive markets; the EU pursues innovation and strategic autonomy. Classify the **specific legal arrangement**, then identify pressure from rival models. A hybrid may signal adaptation rather than inconsistency.

Name the presumptively trusted governor, the value placed first, and the legal instrument. Then test whether security or industrial policy has displaced the model's ordinary commitments. State its productive strength and characteristic failure together.

## II. The Brussels Effect: when domestic regulation becomes global conduct

### 1. Definition and causal chain

The **Brussels Effect** is the EU's unilateral capacity to regulate global markets through the interaction of domestic rules and private firms' incentives. It does not require a treaty, foreign consent, or an explicit EU campaign to export law.

Keep its causal chain explicit:

`EU rule → access to a valuable market requires compliance → regulated target cannot be moved away → separate production or compliance is impractical or uneconomic → firm adopts the strict standard more broadly → practice may later be copied into foreign law`

If one link is missing, do not invoke “the Brussels Effect” as a synonym for influence.

### 2. The five enabling conditions

All five conditions should be assessed; market size alone is insufficient.

#### Market size

A large and affluent consumer market raises the opportunity cost of exit. The relevant inquiry is not aggregate GDP in the abstract but the regulated firm's dependence on the market for the particular product, service, users, or revenue at issue. Alternatives matter: a producer with substitutable demand elsewhere has more leverage than one that cannot forgo European consumers.

#### Regulatory capacity

The jurisdiction must be able to formulate, administer, monitor, and enforce complex rules. Capacity includes legal competence, specialist expertise, budget, information, sanctioning authority, judicial support, and the political willingness to use them. In the EU, supranational institutions built for market integration supply an unusually developed regulatory machine. Capacity is policy-specific: competence in product safety does not automatically establish competence in every digital domain.

#### Stringent standards

A jurisdiction externalizes a standard only when its rule is materially more demanding than relevant alternatives. Stringency can arise from public preferences, distrust of unregulated markets, precautionary administration, and institutional processes that favor ex ante rules. Compare obligations, enforcement, and practical burden; formal language alone can exaggerate or conceal stringency.

#### Inelastic targets

The object of regulation must be tied to the territory or market in a way firms cannot cheaply relocate. Consumers are paradigmatic: a company can move capital or a factory more easily than it can move European customers out of Europe. Digital services complicate but do not erase territorial attachment; firms still seek users, advertising markets, transactions, and data connected to jurisdictions.

#### Non-divisibility

The firm must find it legally, technically, or economically preferable to follow one standard beyond the regulating market.

- **Legal non-divisibility**: law or legal risk makes separation unavailable or unreliable.
- **Technical non-divisibility**: product architecture, data systems, supply chains, interoperability, or safety design make variants difficult.
- **Economic non-divisibility**: separate versions are possible but duplicative design, production, training, contracting, and monitoring cost more than global conformity.

Non-divisibility is a firm-level decision, not a permanent property of a technology. Modular architecture, geofencing, differentiated terms, or falling customization costs can make a previously non-divisible practice divisible. Conversely, integrated data pipelines and global platforms can increase non-divisibility.

### 3. De facto and de jure Brussels Effects

- **De facto Brussels Effect**: market actors extend an EU-compliant product, process, or policy beyond the EU because uniform compliance serves their interests. The immediate carrier is private choice under market constraint.
- **De jure Brussels Effect**: foreign governments or institutions adopt EU-style rules. This may follow lobbying by firms already bearing the compliance cost, legislative borrowing, learning, emulation, litigation, standard-setting, or the perceived legitimacy of the EU model.

Do not treat de facto conformity as proof of foreign legal agreement. A company may standardize globally while lobbying against the rule and while foreign voters and lawmakers reject its premises. Conversely, legal borrowing can occur without full firm-level convergence when enforcement or implementation differs.

### 4. The “level playing field” feedback loop

Once a multinational has invested in compliance, it may favor extending similar obligations to rivals. A once-opposed firm can become a constituency for de jure convergence because uniform rules reduce competitors' ability to undercut it. This does not make the borrowed rule illegitimate, but it changes the political-economy account: rights language and incumbent cost recovery can operate together.

### 5. Why Brussels, not merely Washington or Beijing

The EU combines a large market with a dense regulatory state, a preference for relatively stringent rules, consumer-facing targets, and circumstances favoring non-divisible compliance. The United States' global companies can export practices, but that is often **corporate or market power**, not the externalization of a stringent public rule. China can export infrastructure and standards, but its mechanism is commonly state-backed construction and dependency rather than a foreign firm's decision to globalize domestic compliance.

The labels therefore identify different causal mechanisms:

| Mechanism | Primary carrier | Immediate source of constraint | Typical path |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| American market power | globally dominant firms | private platform or product architecture | adoption of a firm's terms, defaults, and ecosystem |
| Chinese infrastructure power | state-supported vendors and finance | installed systems and state relationships | procurement, construction, training, dependency |
| European regulatory power | public rules interacting with firms | market-access condition | globalized compliance, then possible legal borrowing |

## III. Testing regulatory reach in a concrete matter

### Step 1 — specify the rule and target

Avoid asking whether “the GDPR,” “the AI Act,” or “EU tech law” has global reach in general. Identify the exact obligation, covered actor, product layer, business process, and enforcement hook. A disclosure obligation may divide cleanly by jurisdiction while a model-development control may not.

### Step 2 — measure market dependence

Identify European revenue, users, strategic importance, network effects, and credible substitutes. Ask what exit would cost and whether the firm can abandon the regulated segment rather than the whole market.

### Step 3 — audit capacity and enforcement

Separate legislative ambition from administrative capacity. Identify regulator, investigatory power, information asymmetry, sanction, litigation path, member-state implementation, and enforcement history. A maximum fine is not capacity by itself.

### Step 4 — compare stringency

Choose realistic comparator regimes and compare effective duties. Include procedural burdens, remedies, and enforcement probability. A formally broad rule may be less stringent in operation than a narrower rule backed by reliable enforcement.

### Step 5 — test elasticity

Can the regulated conduct, asset, user interaction, or business line be relocated or withdrawn? If the target is a local consumer base, exit may be commercially unacceptable. If the target is capital, compute, or a corporate domicile, mobility may be greater.

### Step 6 — disaggregate non-divisibility

For each compliance element, ask separately whether global extension is legally required, technically embedded, economically attractive, reputationally valuable, or strategically chosen. Do not infer technical necessity from a firm's preference for one global policy.

### Step 7 — identify the externalization channel

The outcome may be global product redesign, a common contract, a worldwide moderation standard, a supply-chain mandate, lobbying for foreign legislation, or no export at all. Name the channel and the population it reaches.

### Step 8 — run the welfare and legitimacy ledger

List rights gains, compliance and innovation costs, distribution between incumbents and entrants, foreign democratic-autonomy effects, and any protectionist advantage. State whose perspective supports each conclusion.

## IV. Digital empires: rivalry at two levels

### 1. Horizontal battles among governments

Horizontal rivalry concerns which jurisdiction's model organizes the digital economy. It includes standards, trade, data flows, export controls, supply chains, industrial subsidies, security alliances, and normative appeal.

- **US–China technological supremacy**: security concerns and dependence on critical technologies drive export controls, investment restrictions, localization, and subsidies. Measures may protect genuine security interests while accelerating techno-nationalism and fragmentation.
- **US–EU regulatory conflict**: privacy versus surveillance, content governance, competition policy, and digital taxation expose different rights and market commitments. Cooperation against authoritarianism does not erase these disputes.
- **Model competition**: other states do not merely choose rules; they choose packages of infrastructure, finance, market access, security relationships, and political values.

### 2. Vertical battles between governments and technology firms

Technology companies possess capital, expertise, data, infrastructure, user communities, and control over public discourse. They can help states achieve policy goals while resisting regulation. This creates a vertical contest inside every model.

Analyze:

- **rule-making power** — who writes the operative standard: legislature, regulator, court, platform policy team, or technical designer?
- **information power** — can government verify compliance without relying on the regulated firm?
- **exit and leverage** — can a platform withdraw a service, block news, redesign a feature, or mobilize users against regulation?
- **procedural asymmetry** — do due-process safeguards and fragmented agencies slow public enforcement while firms act globally and immediately?
- **co-option** — is the state using a dominant firm to enforce policy, thereby entrenching the very private power it seeks to govern?

A government can win horizontally by promoting national champions yet lose vertically by making itself dependent on them. Conversely, restraining a domestic firm can improve democratic control while weakening geopolitical leverage. State both sides.

### 3. Intersecting battles and regulatory restraint

Horizontal rivalry often softens vertical regulation: a government may tolerate concentration or surveillance because it wants its firms to compete with a foreign power. The same interaction can harden regulation when foreign ownership is recast as national-security risk. Ask whether “innovation” and “security” are serving as principled grounds, proxies for national advantage, or bargaining tools.

### 4. Expansion through three carriers

- **US — firms and markets**: platforms export defaults, moderation systems, advertising models, and contractual governance. Private power can spread even as the public model loses normative attraction.
- **China — infrastructure**: networks, cloud systems, surveillance tools, finance, and technical assistance produce installed dependencies and enable state-centered governance.
- **EU — regulation**: legal obligations become worldwide business practices through the Brussels Effect and then possible templates for foreign law.

Expansion is not binary. Recipient states selectively combine elements, and the same state may use European privacy rules, Chinese infrastructure, and American platforms.

## V. Limits, counterforces, and future conditions

### Counterforces to the Brussels Effect

- declining relative market importance;
- rising alternative markets;
- technological modularity that makes compliance divisible;
- successful foreign counter-regulation or blocking statutes;
- weak or uneven enforcement;
- standards set earlier by another power or private consortium;
- a rule whose costs provoke exit rather than conformity;
- geopolitical fragmentation that replaces global products with regional stacks.

Technological change cuts both ways. Customization and geofencing can weaken global standardization; shared cloud architecture, foundation models, integrated supply chains, and reputational commitments can strengthen it.

### Enforcement as the decisive vertical question

Formal enactment can overstate public power. Large technology firms can exploit delay, appeal, fragmented jurisdiction, and information asymmetry. Evaluate whether the regulator can obtain evidence, compel technical change, monitor recurrence, and impose remedies at a scale that changes incentives. If not, describe the outcome as symbolic, partial, or negotiated rather than a government victory.

### Innovation and regime-type claims

Do not accept either “regulation kills innovation” or “rights regulation is costless.” Separate invention from commercialization, entrants from incumbents, compliance from fragmentation costs, and innovation's rate from its direction. Likewise, a **techno-democracy** versus **techno-autocracy** frame is not a moral shortcut: democratic states surveil and tolerate private domination, while authoritarian infrastructure may meet real development needs. Ask which institutions make power contestable, attributable, and rights-constrained in practice.

## VI. Normative-restraint ledger

After diagnosing capacity and reach, stage the strongest objections to the apparent winner.

| Claim | Serious objection | What must be shown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Globalized EU rule protects rights | It overrides choices of people who never voted for EU lawmakers | Why the protected interest and market connection justify extraterritorial practical effects |
| Uniform global compliance is efficient | It eliminates lawful diversity and can lock in an unsuitable rule | Why uniformity's gains exceed experimentation and local preference |
| Stringent standards raise welfare | They may raise prices, impede entry, or entrench incumbents able to absorb fixed costs | Distribution by firm size, consumer group, and jurisdiction |
| Foreign adoption validates the model | Borrowing may reflect lobbying, dependency, or lack of alternatives | The causal path and quality of domestic deliberation |
| Infrastructure promotes development | It can produce surveillance capacity and long-term dependency | Governance safeguards, procurement choice, data control, and exit options |
| National champions protect sovereignty | They can defeat domestic accountability and privatize public decisions | Whether government can regulate the champion it relies on |
| Security requires decoupling | Security can become a cover for protectionism and techno-nationalism | Threat model, tailoring, spillovers, and less restrictive alternatives |

Use Bradford's disciplined conclusion: effects create winners and losers, so whether they are beneficial depends on whom one asks. Name the relevant constituencies rather than using the phrase as a ritual disclaimer.

## VII. Decision rules

- When asked whether a foreign firm “must follow EU law everywhere,” separate legal territorial scope from de facto global operational conformity.
- When a rule is predicted to travel, require a complete causal chain through market dependence, capacity, stringency, elasticity, and non-divisibility.
- When a company adopts one global standard, do not infer agreement with the rule; test cost, architecture, reputation, and strategic lobbying.
- When another jurisdiction copies EU language, do not infer equivalent protection; compare implementation, institutions, remedies, and enforcement.
- When the US invokes innovation, test whether the claim protects contestable experimentation or entrenched private governors.
- When China exports a system, examine the surrounding finance, standards, training, data access, and maintenance—not only the hardware.
- When the EU asserts rights, test whether enforcement redistributes power or merely creates compliance procedures.
- When national security appears, trace how horizontal rivalry changes domestic willingness to discipline technology firms.
- When assessing a “winner” among models, report separately technological capacity, regulatory reach, democratic legitimacy, rights protection, and vertical control.
- When benefits are claimed, identify winners, losers, foreign autonomy costs, entry effects, and the perspective from which “beneficial” is being judged.

## Key takeaways

1. A regulatory design is a map of institutional trust and power before it is a solution to a technical problem.
2. The market-driven, state-driven, and rights-driven models are diagnostic defaults; real systems are hybrids under pressure.
3. The Brussels Effect is a five-condition mechanism, not a synonym for EU influence or extraterritorial jurisdiction.
4. De facto firm behavior and de jure foreign law are distinct outcomes with different evidence requirements.
5. Digital power operates horizontally among states and vertically between states and technology companies.
6. The US chiefly exports through firms, China through infrastructure, and the EU through regulation, but recipient states mix all three.
7. Reach, legitimacy, enforcement, innovation, and welfare must be assessed separately.
8. Every favorable conclusion owes an account of protectionism, incumbent advantage, foreign democratic autonomy, and who bears the cost.
