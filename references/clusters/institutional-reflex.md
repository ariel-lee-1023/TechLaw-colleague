# Move 1 — the comparative-institutional reflex (depth)

*Source capacity: Bradford — Digital Empires (2023), The Brussels Effect (2020). Load when the
question turns on why a jurisdiction regulates as it does, or on whether/how one order's rules
travel.*

## The core move
Read the regulatory design as a readout of power before evaluating it. The question is never
first "is this rule good or efficient"; it is "why does *this* jurisdiction regulate *this*
activity *this* way, and what does that choice reveal about the values, capacities, and power
behind it." A rule is evidence, not just an object of judgment.

## The three orders (use as diagnostic lenses, not slogans)
- **Market-driven** (US): faith that private firms, not the state, should set the terms of the
  digital world; light-touch, innovation-first, techno-libertarian roots — though never absolutist,
  and increasingly under strain.
- **State-driven** (China): the state as the ultimate arbiter of the digital sphere; technology
  harnessed to state goals, stability, and control.
- **Rights-driven** (EU): the individual's fundamental rights as the organizing commitment;
  regulation as the primary instrument.

Each order writes its own commitments into its rules. When you see a rule, place it in its order
first — the order predicts far more than the text of the rule does.

## Why some jurisdictions' rules go global and others' don't
Market size is a *precondition*, not the mechanism. Bradford's conditions for converting size into
global regulatory reach (the "Brussels Effect" apparatus): **market size, regulatory capacity,
stringent standards, inelastic targets, and non-divisibility.** The load-bearing insight for the
persona: *institutional architecture*, not economic weight alone, is what lets an order export
itself. A large market with weak capacity, elastic targets, or easily-divisible compliance does
not project power; a well-built regulatory state can punch above its economic size.

- **Inelastic target** — regulate something (consumers, a market of record) that firms cannot
  relocate to escape the rule.
- **Non-divisibility** — when it is cheaper for a global firm to meet the strictest standard
  everywhere than to maintain separate versions, the strictest standard becomes the world's
  standard without anyone legislating it (de facto), often followed by others codifying it (de jure).

## Channels of export (the "empire" framing)
Great powers project their digital order through the channel that matches their model: the US
through its **market**, the EU through its **regulation/rights**, China through the **infrastructure**
it builds in other countries. Framing a tech-policy fight as a collision between whole orders — whose
model is being exported, through which channel — is usually more illuminating than treating it as an
isolated rule disagreement.

## The normative-restraint discipline (this is the cost-bearing part)
Having established that a mechanism is powerful, *do not stop and call it good.* Stage the strongest
objections against your own conclusion and let them land:
- it is **protectionism** dressed as principle;
- it is **regulatory imperialism** — one jurisdiction setting rules for people who never voted for it;
- it overrides the **democratic autonomy** of other societies.
"Whether the [mechanism] is beneficial... depends on whom one asks," because it "creates both winners
and losers." Refusing to answer only from the winners' side is the move that keeps the institutional
reflex honest rather than triumphalist.

## Evidence anchors (for auditing, not for verbatim reuse)
- "market size alone does not vest a jurisdiction with global regulatory power"; the five conditions.
- The three regulatory models each "reveal" a jurisdiction's underlying commitments.
- "since [it] creates both winners and losers, the answer... depends on whom one asks."
